# Research Paper Report for 2026-09-01:2026-09-15

## Overall Summary

The papers in this report bridge artificial-intelligence methodology and molecular biology, with a clear tilt toward using foundation-model-powered tools to accelerate therapeutic discovery. Across four publications, the dominant themes are agentic model development, protein and antibody sequence foundation models, knowledge-graph-enhanced prediction, and the use of language-model-derived priors to clean noisy biological screens. Collectively they illustrate how generative and discriminative AI are moving from proof-of-concept demonstrations into structured scientific workflows—model design, target identification, and mechanism interpretation—while also highlighting unresolved issues such as evaluation on small held-out sets, positive-unlabelled learning assumptions, and the need for clinician-grounded validation.

The most conceptually ambitious entry is [1], which turns an LLM agent into the architect of a deep-learning model for RNA 3D structure prediction. Rather than merely prompting a model for an answer, the authors let the agent propose, implement, train, and evaluate model edits across 297 iterations under a fixed budget and human supervision. The result is QuickFold, an 8.9-million-parameter folding trunk that reportedly reaches parity with open-source leaders RhoFold+ and NuFold on lDDT and TM-score while using much less inference compute. Methodologically, this is a case study in feedback-driven, agent-led model development, not just a new predictor. Its main limitation is the same as any iterative self-improvement loop: success depends on the quality of the fitness signal (here lDDT/TM-score) and the risk of overfitting to a single held-out test set. The budget-constrained framing, however, makes the work more reproducible than unconstrained “research assistant” demos.

The two foundation-model papers, [2] and [3], take very different directions. [2] introduces a foundation model trained on fully human heavy-chain-only antibodies, a class of therapeutically attractive but under-modeled molecules. Details are sparse because the preprint lacks an abstract, but the stated goal is to learn the sequence and functional grammar of these antibodies, which could underpin generative design or paratope/epitope modeling. [3] uses an existing protein language model to score protein–protein interactions and refine a genome-wide RNA-interference screen of vaccinia virus host factors. The ICARus framework treats true virus-host interactions as a positive-unlabelled learning problem, integrating PLM-derived interaction priors to reduce off-target and assay-noise artifacts. Compared with [2], which is model-centric, [3] is workflow-centric: it shows how a pre-trained PLM can be wrapped into an agentic-AI-ready screening pipeline.

Finally, [4] applies LLM-driven knowledge-graph learning to traditional Chinese medicine (TCM), predicting herb–macromolecule target interactions through a framework called LLM-HTI. It fuses LLM-extracted semantic representations with the topology of a Traditional Chinese and Western medicine knowledge graph (TWKG) and introduces a gradient-guided adaptive parameter update mechanism to improve training stability. What distinguishes [4] from the others is its explicit human-in-the-loop validation: gastrointestinal surgeons rank candidates across disease relevance, biological plausibility, clinical actionability, and translational priority.

Taken together, the papers reveal a trend: AI is not only predicting molecular structures and interactions but is increasingly used to design the predictors themselves, curate the training data, and contextualize predictions for downstream therapeutic decisions. Yet the field still faces the usual translational hurdles—reproducible benchmarks, sparse labels, biological validation beyond computational metrics, and the opacity of foundation-model representations.

## AI Agents

This category contains one paper that explores the frontier of agentic AI in model development. The work moves beyond using LLMs as chat interfaces or search tools and instead treats a general-purpose LLM agent as the principal engineer of a scientific predictor. The chosen problem, RNA 3D structure prediction, is genuinely hard: RNA fold space is large, experimental structures are scarcer than protein structures, and standard accuracy metrics such as lDDT and TM-score are sensitive to small geometric errors. The authors construct a supervised loop in which the agent iteratively proposes architectural or training changes, implements them, trains the resulting model, and evaluates it against a held-out test set. Across 297 iterations the system evolves a randomly initialized baseline into QuickFold, an 8.9 M-parameter folding trunk. The reported result is competitive with RhoFold+ and NuFold, the strongest open-source RNA folding baselines, within noise on lDDT and TM-score, while offering a fraction of the inference cost. Methodologically, the paper is valuable because it frames the contribution as a case study in feedback-driven development rather than a pure architecture paper. Limitations include the reliance on a single held-out test set for the fitness signal, the possibility that the agent is exploiting narrow metric improvements rather than generalizing to new RNA folds, and the need for careful human supervision to stay within the fixed budget. Nonetheless, the study suggests a plausible workflow for automating parts of model development in structural biology.

## Foundation models

The foundation-models category includes two bioRxiv preprints that apply large pre-trained models to antibody sequences and virus-host interactions, respectively. Together they show both ends of the foundation-model spectrum: building a new domain-specific model versus repurposing an existing protein language model to clean and prioritize experimental readouts.

Paper [2] targets fully human heavy-chain-only antibodies, an emerging class of biologics with favorable developability and tissue-penetration properties. The authors propose a foundation model that learns the sequence and functional grammar of these molecules. Because the abstract is not available in the provided metadata, technical specifics—such as architecture, training data size, tokenizer design, and evaluation benchmarks—cannot be assessed here. The stated motivation, however, is clear: antibody drug discovery has historically relied on animal immunization or display libraries, and a generative or predictive foundation model trained on fully human sequences could streamline hit identification and humanization.

Paper [3], in contrast, does not train a new foundation model but uses a protein language model to derive protein–protein interaction information for refining a genome-wide RNA-interference screen of vaccinia virus host factors. The biological context is timely because poxvirus outbreaks, including mpox, have renewed interest in host-directed antivirals. RNAi screens are noisy: off-target effects and assay variability obscure true gene-phenotype relationships. The authors introduce ICARus, a positive-unlabelled read-out refinement framework that integrates PLM-derived interaction priors. The method enhances the discovery of human genes with potential antiviral function and releases the raw and refined screen readouts as an agentic-AI-enabled community resource. While [2] is model-centric, [3] is workflow-centric; [3] also has a clearer empirical validation story because it starts from an existing high-throughput dataset.

A comparative limitation is that [2] lacks disclosed evaluation metrics in the available metadata, whereas [3] must contend with the inherent bias of positive-unlabelled learning—undiscovered interactions are treated as negatives, and PLM interaction scores may carry their own training-set biases. Both papers nevertheless support the broader trend of using foundation models to compress biological sequence and interaction knowledge into reusable priors for drug-discovery tasks.

## Other Models

This category includes a single interdisciplinary paper that combines large language models, knowledge graphs, and clinician validation to predict herb–macromolecule target interactions. Traditional Chinese medicine (TCM) is a compelling testbed because herbal formulations are inherently multi-component and multi-target, but the systems-level mechanisms behind their efficacy are difficult to decode with reductionist assays alone. The authors present LLM-HTI, an LLM-driven graph-learning framework that operates on a Traditional Chinese and Western medicine knowledge graph (TWKG). The framework first extracts latent semantic representations of herb-target associations using LLMs, then integrates these embeddings with the TWKG topology to jointly characterize herbs, macromolecular targets, and their functional relationships. To improve training dynamics, the authors introduce a gradient-guided adaptive parameter-updating mechanism that dynamically selects important parameter subsets during optimization. This mechanism is intended to stabilize training and improve optimization efficiency relative to standard full-parameter updates.

What sets [4] apart from the other papers in this report is its explicit translational validation layer. Gastrointestinal surgeons independently assessed top-ranked candidate targets for disease relevance, biological plausibility, clinical actionability, and translational priority. This clinician-in-the-loop evaluation addresses a common weakness in computational drug-discovery papers: high-ranking predictions that look good in silico but lack biological or clinical credibility. On the technical side, limitations include potential gaps in the TWKG coverage, the risk that LLM embeddings inherit biomedical biases or outdated knowledge, and the need for prospective experimental validation beyond retrospective benchmarks and expert review. The availability of data and code at the reported GitHub repository improves reproducibility. Overall, [4] exemplifies how AI models can be embedded into broader discovery pipelines that combine structured knowledge, representation learning, and domain-expert judgment.
