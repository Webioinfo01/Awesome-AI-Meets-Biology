# Research Paper Report for 2026-03-01:2026-03-31

## Overall Summary

This report synthesizes **22 research papers** published between March 1–31, 2026, spanning five thematic categories: AI Agents, Benchmarks, Foundation Models, Reviews, and Databases. The collection reveals several dominant trends shaping the current landscape of large language model (LLM) research.

**LLMs as Clinical Decision-Support and Workflow Agents.** A major theme is the deployment of LLMs as autonomous or semi-autonomous agents within specialized professional domains. Geo-Commander [1] introduces a novel ReAct-based spatial reasoning framework for combat simulation, integrating hexagonal grid encoding to address LLM deficiencies in dynamic geospatial tasks. UltraReporter [6] demonstrates a compact 8B-parameter LLM pipeline that converts spoken diagnostic cues into structured ultrasound reports, achieving superior clinical quality scores over chief physicians in blinded evaluation. In ophthalmology, [3] evaluates LLM-based scribing tools using simulated clinical encounters, while [4] presents a cognitive layer architecture to enhance LLM performance in psychotherapy interactions. The psychotherapy framework [4], published in *Nature Medicine*, is particularly notable for its attempt to bridge the gap between general-purpose LLMs and the nuanced demands of mental health care. In drug design, LLMsFold [5] integrates LLMs with biophysical simulations for *de novo* molecular generation, pointing toward agentic scientific discovery pipelines. Meanwhile, [2] evaluates a locally deployed 20-billion parameter model for automated abstract screening in systematic reviews, addressing practical concerns around data privacy and computational accessibility.

**Rigorous Benchmarking Across Medical Specialties.** The benchmarking studies collectively demonstrate that while LLMs show promising performance in clinical scenarios, their capabilities vary substantially by model, task complexity, and medical domain. In pituitary adenoma management [7], Claude Opus 4.1 outperformed ChatGPT-5.0 and Gemini 2.5 Flash across quality, reasoning, and satisfaction metrics using validated clinician-based instruments (QAMAI, AIPI, AISPE-Q). A sleep medicine study [11] evaluated nine LLM configurations on 197 certification-aligned questions, finding that premium models (Gemini 2.5 Pro at 95.9%) significantly outperform free alternatives, raising equity concerns. In colorectal cancer [9], three LLMs showed only fair agreement (62.5–65.4%) with expert multidisciplinary team decisions, performing better in standardized, less complex cases. The neuropsychological evaluation study [10] found ChatGPT-5 achieved 78% accuracy in classifying idiopathic normal pressure hydrocephalus from standardized test summaries, with meaningful prognostic signal (83% accuracy). Bronchiolitis surveillance [8] demonstrated that both deep learning and GPT-5 outperform traditional ML models and ICD-9 coding for classifying diagnoses from emergency department free text.

**Foundation Models for Scientific Discovery.** The foundation model papers highlight rapid progress in domain-specific architectures. ToothXpert [12] introduces a multimodal LLM for dental orthopantomography analysis, achieving a macro F1 of 78.61% and outperforming both general and medical MLLMs. CellVQ [15] presents a single-cell foundation model trained on 68 million cells with 500 million parameters, introducing a novel Single-Cell Discretization module for interpretability. The paired Protein Pair Language Model (PPLM) [16] jointly encodes protein sequences for interaction prediction, achieving state-of-the-art performance in binding affinity modeling and surpassing ESM2 and structure-based methods. RNAElectra [14] adapts the ELECTRA architecture for RNA regulatory inference. In mathematics, [13] applies BERT-based transformers to mathematical entity relation extraction with SHAP-based explainability, reaching 99.39% accuracy.

**Critical Reviews on AI Safety, Ethics, and Methodology.** The reviews raise crucial concerns about LLM deployment. A *Lancet Psychiatry* article [17] examines how agential AI might co-create or amplify delusions in vulnerable users, proposing AI-informed care protocols with digital advance statements and escalation safeguards. A *JAMA Psychiatry* viewpoint [21] explores risks and opportunities of AI in mental health beyond LLMs. The radiology review [20] addresses how LLM labeling errors can distort AI performance evaluations, while [19] from the Society of Technology in Anesthesia contextualizes AI evaluation metrics across clinical use cases.

**Data Infrastructure.** A single database paper, h5adify [22], introduces neuro-symbolic metadata harmonization for scalable AnnData integration using local LLMs, addressing a critical bottleneck in single-cell data interoperability.

Collectively, these papers demonstrate that LLMs are transitioning from experimental tools to practical clinical and scientific agents, but performance is highly variable, and rigorous evaluation frameworks, interpretability mechanisms, and safety guardrails remain essential before widespread deployment.

## AI Agents

The AI Agents category encompasses six papers [1]–[6] that explore LLM-powered autonomous systems across diverse domains, from military combat simulation to clinical documentation and scientific discovery. A unifying challenge across these works is bridging the gap between LLMs' text-based reasoning and the demands of real-world, domain-specific tasks that require spatial understanding, multimodal input processing, or integration with specialized computational pipelines.

Geo-Commander [1] directly addresses the well-known limitation of LLMs in spatial reasoning by introducing hexagonal grid encoding and a ReAct-based reasoning chain for combat simulation point selection. While most prior work treats LLM spatial reasoning as an incidental capability, [1] makes it a central architectural concern, achieving consistent improvements in selection quality and win rate in tank detachment simulations. This contrasts with UltraReporter [6], which focuses on temporal real-time processing—converting spoken ultrasound cues into structured reports. Where [1] emphasizes spatial encoding, [6] leverages a multi-agent synthesis framework to overcome data scarcity and employs defect-oriented preference optimization to minimize hallucinations, achieving reports that surpass chief physicians in 72% of prospective evaluations. Both papers demonstrate that domain-specific architectural adaptations (spatial encoding for combat, template-augmented fine-tuning for radiology) are critical for deploying LLMs beyond general text tasks.

The medical agent papers [3], [4] tackle clinical documentation and psychotherapy, respectively. While [3] evaluates existing LLM-based scribing tools for ophthalmology encounters—a benchmarking-oriented approach—[4] proposes a novel cognitive layer architecture for psychotherapy, published in the high-impact *Nature Medicine*. The cognitive layer approach in [4] likely addresses the specific reasoning demands of therapeutic dialogue that standard LLM prompting cannot satisfy. LLMsFold [5] takes LLMs into computational biology, integrating them with biophysical simulations for de novo drug design, representing an agentic scientific workflow where LLMs serve as orchestrators of complex simulation pipelines. Finally, [2] evaluates a locally deployed 20-billion parameter model for systematic review automation, addressing practical deployment concerns (data privacy, computational cost) that are often overlooked in cloud-centric LLM evaluations.

## Benchmarks

The Benchmarks category includes five papers [7]–[11] that systematically evaluate LLM performance across a spectrum of medical specialties: pituitary adenoma management, bronchiolitis surveillance, colorectal cancer multidisciplinary decision-making, neuropsychological diagnostics, and sleep medicine certification. These studies collectively reveal important patterns regarding model variability, task complexity, and evaluation methodology.

A critical finding across these benchmarks is the significant inter-model performance variability. In the pituitary adenoma study [7], Claude Opus 4.1 achieved the highest aggregate QAMAI score (4.39 ± 0.66), outperforming ChatGPT-5.0 (4.12 ± 0.74) and Gemini 2.5 Flash (4.07 ± 0.76) with statistical significance (p = 0.018). This contrasts with the sleep medicine study [11], where Gemini 2.5 Pro led at 95.9% accuracy, followed by Claude Opus 4 (93.9%) and GPT-4o (93.4%). The rank order of models shifts across domains, suggesting that no single LLM is universally superior and that domain-specific evaluation is essential before clinical adoption. The sleep medicine study [11] is particularly methodologically rigorous, employing a strict 3/3 concordance criterion across 197 questions posed three times each, and demonstrating that scoring methodology (single-try vs. majority voting vs. strict concordance) has minimal impact on model rankings (Spearman's ρ = 0.879–1.000).

The colorectal cancer study [9] offers a sobering counterpoint, finding only fair agreement (62.5–65.4%) between LLMs and expert multidisciplinary teams. Multivariable analysis of the most stable model (O3, with 81% intramodel agreement) identified treatment-naïve status, non-metastatic disease, and colon tumor location as predictors of higher concordance, suggesting LLMs perform better in more standardized clinical scenarios but struggle with complex, multi-factorial treatment planning. Similarly, the neuropsychological study [10] found diagnostic accuracy ranging from 63% to 78% across three LLMs when classifying idiopathic normal pressure hydrocephalus from standardized test summaries, with ChatGPT-5 achieving the best performance (AUC = 0.84). Its exploratory prognostic analysis showed 83% accuracy for predicting postoperative shunt responsiveness, hinting at practical clinical utility.

The bronchiolitis surveillance study [8] is unique in comparing LLMs not just against clinical experts but against traditional machine learning and deep learning models. GPT-5 (F1 = 0.99, balanced accuracy = 0.99) and deep learning (F1 = 0.96, balanced accuracy = 0.98) significantly outperformed classical ML models (F1 = 0.86–0.96) and ICD-9 administrative coding (sensitivity = 85.9%). This demonstrates LLMs' strength in natural language understanding of unstructured clinical text, a task where their pre-training on large text corpora provides a natural advantage.

## Foundation Models

The Foundation Models category features five papers [12]–[16] spanning dental imaging, mathematical reasoning, RNA biology, single-cell genomics, and protein-protein interactions. These works collectively illustrate two key trends: the development of domain-specific foundation models that dramatically outperform general-purpose counterparts, and the integration of interpretability mechanisms into foundation model architectures.

ToothXpert [12] exemplifies the first trend, achieving a macro F1 of 78.61% on orthopantomography analysis—outperforming LLaVA v.1.5 by 23.19%, LLaVA-Med v.1.5 by 41.39%, Qwen-VL by 56.38%, and HuatuoGPT-Vision by 26.74%. This substantial margin underscores that domain-specific curating of alignment datasets (MM-OPG with 131,065 QA pairs) and task-specific fine-tuning yield dramatic improvements over generic multimodal architectures. ToothXpert also matched or exceeded junior dentists (3 years experience) on external validation by 1.96–2.99% F1 while requiring significantly less inference time. In the biological sciences, CellVQ [15] represents a major advance in single-cell foundation models, training on 68 million cells with 500 million parameters and introducing a novel Single-Cell Discretization (SCD) module that transforms sparse, high-dimensional data into interpretable "cell codes." While [12] focuses on clinical deployment with visual and language outputs, [15] emphasizes interpretability and knowledge graph construction (CellVQ-Graph) for biological discovery—representing complementary approaches to making foundation models actionable.

The protein-protein interaction model PPLM [16] addresses a fundamental limitation of existing protein language models: their design for individual chains rather than paired sequences. By jointly encoding paired protein sequences, PPLM-PPI achieves state-of-the-art performance in binary interaction prediction across species, while PPLM-Affinity outperforms both ESM2 and structure-based methods, particularly on challenging antibody–antigen and TCR–pMHC complexes. This contrasts with RNAElectra [14], which adapts the ELECTRA discriminator-style pre-training for RNA regulatory inference—representing a more conservative architectural approach applied to a different biological modality. Both [15] and [16] are published in *Nature Communications*, reflecting the increasing recognition of biological foundation models in top-tier venues.

The mathematical reasoning work [13] stands apart from the biological and clinical papers by focusing on mathematical entity relation extraction, formulating problem interpretation as a MERE task. BERT achieves 99.39% accuracy, with SHAP-based explainability analysis providing insights into feature importance. While [13] uses a relatively simpler architecture compared to the large-scale models in [15] and [16], its emphasis on transparent AI and interpretability aligns with a growing demand for explainable models across all domains.

## Reviews

The Reviews category comprises five papers [17]–[21] that critically examine the broader implications of LLM deployment in healthcare, with particular emphasis on safety, evaluation methodology, and ethical considerations. While the benchmarking and agent papers in this report focus on what LLMs *can* do, these reviews address what LLMs *should* do—and the risks when they are deployed without adequate safeguards.

The most provocative contribution is the *Lancet Psychiatry* personal view [17], which examines how agential AI interactions might co-create or amplify delusions in users with psychotic disorders. This paper [17] proposes that LLMs' tendency to validate user input—their "helpful assistant" training—poses a unique risk for individuals with delusional or grandiose content, potentially reinforcing epistemic instability and blurring reality boundaries. The authors propose a framework of "AI-informed care" including personalized instruction protocols, reflective check-ins, digital advance statements, and escalation safeguards, reframing the AI agent as an "epistemic ally" rather than a therapist. This is complemented by the *JAMA Psychiatry* viewpoint [21], which broadens the discussion beyond LLMs to all AI in mental health care, arguing that successful risk management would allow both patients and clinicians to experience AI benefits. Where [17] focuses on the specific mechanisms of delusion co-creation, [21] addresses the systemic risks and opportunities at a policy level.

The evaluation methodology reviews [19] and [20] raise important methodological concerns. The Society of Technology in Anesthesia expert statement [19] contextualizes AI evaluation metrics across clinical use cases in anesthesiology, arguing that standard metrics may not adequately capture clinical utility. This connects directly to [20], published in *Radiology: Artificial Intelligence*, which demonstrates how labeling errors by LLMs can distort downstream AI performance evaluations—a meta-level concern that affects the validity of all benchmarking studies, including those in this report [7]–[11]. If LLMs are used to generate training labels or evaluation datasets, their errors propagate systematically through the pipeline.

The *Nature Reviews Nephrology* article [18] provides a broad overview of LLMs in healthcare, serving as a foundational reference for the clinical audiences that most of the empirical papers in this report target. Its placement in a specialty review journal suggests that awareness of LLM capabilities and limitations is reaching domain specialists, not just the AI research community.

## Databases

The Databases category contains a single paper [22] that addresses a critical but often overlooked aspect of AI infrastructure: data interoperability and metadata harmonization. h5adify introduces a neuro-symbolic approach to harmonizing metadata in AnnData files—the standard format for single-cell genomics data—using local large language models.

This work is directly relevant to the foundation model papers in this report, particularly CellVQ [15] which trains on 68 million cells from diverse datasets. A persistent challenge in single-cell biology is that metadata labels (cell types, tissue origins, experimental conditions) are inconsistently formatted across datasets, making large-scale integration error-prone. h5adify's neuro-symbolic approach—combining symbolic reasoning for schema mapping with LLM-based natural language understanding—offers a principled solution. The use of local LLMs is notable: it addresses data privacy concerns inherent in genomic datasets while also enabling deployment in resource-constrained environments, echoing the motivations of [2] which evaluates locally deployed LLMs for systematic review automation.

While only one database paper appears in this reporting period, its significance is amplified by its role as enabling infrastructure. Without reliable data harmonization, the foundation models and benchmarks in this report would be limited to curated, manually annotated datasets, constraining their scalability and generalizability.
