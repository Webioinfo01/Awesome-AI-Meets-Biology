# Research Paper Report for 2026-09-01:2026-09-15

## Overall Summary

This biweekly report captures a focused but methodologically diverse snapshot of artificial intelligence applications in biomedical and biological research, spanning autonomous LLM agents for scientific discovery, domain-specific foundation models, and AI-augmented knowledge infrastructure. A unifying theme across the seven papers [1]-[7] is the operationalization of large language models (LLMs) and foundation architectures as active research instruments rather than passive predictors, with several works [1], [2], [3], [6] explicitly framing their contributions around agentic or feedback-driven workflows. The strongest single demonstration of this paradigm shift is [1], which describes an LLM agent that drove 297 iterative cycles of model proposal, implementation, training, and evaluation to produce QuickFold—an 8.9 M-parameter RNA 3D folding trunk that matches RhoFold+ and NuFold on lDDT and TM-score within noise at a fraction of inference cost. This case study reframes agentic AI not as a predictor itself but as a development accelerator for hard biological problems, where the scientific bottleneck is search over the hypothesis-model-training space.

The healthcare-knowledge-graph line of work, exemplified by [2] and [3], shares a methodological philosophy: coupling LLMs to curated biomedical knowledge graphs (SPOKE, TWKG) to constrain generation and improve downstream task accuracy. In [2], the MedCP framework integrates an OMOP-standardized EHR (over 7 million subjects) with SPOKE through the Model Context Protocol, and is evaluated across 100 clinical research tasks, 617 BiomixQA factual accuracy questions, an integrative EHR-KG case study, and a standardized replication protocol—achieving task-completion compression from months to hours while leaving study design and interpretation under expert supervision. In [3], the LLM-HTI framework combines LLM-derived latent semantic representations with knowledge-graph topology and introduces gradient-guided adaptive parameter updates to predict herb-macromolecular target interactions, augmented by clinician-guided assessment of top candidates for translational prioritization.

Foundation-model work in this window centers on biology-specific sequence and structure grammars. [4] proposes a foundation model for fully human heavy-chain-only antibodies—addressing the underexplored camelid-inspired antibody space where sequence-function rules differ from conventional antibodies and which is central to next-generation biologic drug discovery. [5] adapts AgroNT, a plant-genome DNA language model, into an environment-aware variant via stress-specific prompt tokens and parameter-efficient fine-tuning, prioritizing heat- and drought-responsive maize loci validated against the G2F panel, spatiotemporal differential expression, and stress QTLs—an instructive template for stress-conditioned genomic foundation models. [6] introduces ICARus, a positive-unlabelled refinement framework that uses protein-language-model-derived interaction signals to denoise a genome-wide vaccinia-host RNA interference screen, sharpening prioritization of antiviral human genes and releasing both raw and refined read-outs as an agentic-AI-ready community resource.

Methodological depth varies but trends toward rigor: several papers [1], [2], [5] report multi-axis evaluation (held-out benchmarks, case studies, downstream biological validation), while [2] and [3] explicitly benchmark multiple foundation-model types (GPT-5.5, Claude Opus 4.8). Knowledge-graph integration is the dominant grounding strategy, appearing in [2], [3], and [7]. Practical implications cluster around three translational vectors—(i) accelerated biologics design (heavy-chain antibodies [4], RNA-targeting [1]), (ii) natural-product and antiviral target identification (herbal macromolecules [3], terpenoid platform [7], poxvirus-host factors [6]), and (iii) democratized clinical evidence generation [2]. Common limitations acknowledged or implied include reliance on pre-trained foundation models with potential knowledge-cutoff drift (addressed via KG grounding in [2], [3], [7]), dependence on curated graphs that may inherit source-database bias, and—as [1] candidly notes—still-required human supervision in the agent loop. The papers collectively suggest that the field is moving from "LLM for biology" toward "agentic, knowledge-grounded systems for biology," with database and platform work [7] providing the connective tissue.

## AI Agents

This category features two papers that operationalize LLM-based agents as autonomous or semi-autonomous drivers of scientific and clinical workflows, with explicit emphasis on human-in-the-loop supervision and feedback-driven iteration. The first work, [1], pushes the agentic paradigm into model-building itself: a general-purpose LLM agent under a fixed budget and human supervision ran 297 iterative proposal-implementation-training-evaluation cycles to evolve an RNA 3D structure predictor from a random initialization to QuickFold (8.9 M parameters), which matches the strongest open-source baselines (RhoFold+, NuFold) on lDDT and TM-score within noise on a held-out test set while consuming only a fraction of the inference cost. The contribution is explicitly framed as a case study in feedback-driven, agent-led model development rather than a state-of-the-art predictor, raising important questions about compute-efficient research ideation, the role of human supervisory signals, and the upper bound of what search-budget-driven agents can achieve on structurally hard biological problems. A limitation worth noting is that the reported metrics are "within noise" of competitors rather than strictly superior, and the approach is single-domain—extensions to multi-modal or multi-objective biological tasks remain open.

The second work, [2], embeds an agentic layer atop real-world clinical infrastructure via the Model Context Protocol (MCP), coupling an OMOP-standardized academic health system EHR (>7 million subjects) with the SPOKE curated knowledge graph to deliver MedCP. Evaluation is unusually thorough: 100 clinical research tasks, 617 factual accuracy items on BiomixQA, an integrative case study linking EHR co-occurrence with molecular-network similarity to surface mechanistic hypotheses, and a standardized protocol for replicating published observational studies, where the system compressed timelines dramatically (months → hours) without displacing expert supervision. Comparative behavior is illuminating—knowledge-graph access improved mean benchmark scores for both GPT-5.5 and Claude Opus 4.8, but the pattern differed between models, and SPOKE grounding boosted multiple-choice accuracy on BiomixQA without changing true/false accuracy, suggesting that KG grounding primarily stabilizes answer-format-bound reasoning rather than binary discrimination. Compared to [1], which uses an agent to *build* a model, [2] uses an agent to *query* and *reason over* existing data infrastructure; together they bracket the spectrum from model-creation agents to data-navigation agents. Limitations common to both include reliance on LLM stochasticity mitigated only by KG constraints, and the continued necessity of expert-in-the-loop governance for study design and interpretation.

## Foundation Models

The four foundation-model papers [3]-[6] share a focus on domain-tailored pretraining or adaptation strategies for biological sequences and interactions, with explicit grounding in functional genomics, antibody engineering, or host-pathogen biology. [3] presents LLM-HTI, a knowledge-graph-driven framework for predicting herb–macromolecular target interactions in the context of Traditional Chinese Medicine; the architecture fuses LLM-extracted latent semantic representations of herb-target associations with knowledge-graph topology and adds a gradient-guided adaptive parameter update mechanism that dynamically selects important parameter subsets to stabilize training. Beyond standard benchmarks, [3] uniquely commissions clinician (gastrointestinal surgeon) evaluation of top-ranked candidates across disease relevance, biological plausibility, clinical actionability, and translational priority—an exemplar of clinician-in-the-loop validation that contrasts with the purely computational validation of [4] and [5].

[4] targets fully human heavy-chain-only antibodies, a relatively underexplored antibody class with distinct sequence-function grammar relative to conventional heterotetrameric antibodies, and which is increasingly relevant for biologic drug discovery due to its compact single-chain architecture. The contribution is a foundation model that learns the sequence and functional grammar of this class, with downstream implications for engineered binders. Compared to [5] and [6], which adapt existing GFMs to new tasks, [4] appears to introduce a class-specific pretraining regime, though the abstract (empty in the metadata) limits deeper methodological commentary. The relationship to [3] is complementary: [3] learns over heterogeneous herb-macromolecule graphs while [4] specializes on a homogeneous but functionally distinct sequence modality.

[5] and [6] together illustrate how existing GFMs can be productively specialized. [5] extends AgroNT—a transformer-based plant-genome foundation model—by injecting stress-specific prompt tokens and applying parameter-efficient fine-tuning to produce environment-aware embeddings that cluster distinctly across heat and drought contexts; prioritized genomic regions in maize are supported by spatiotemporal differential expression, overlap with stress QTLs from the G2F panel, and transcription-factor-family and motif-enrichment analyses. This represents a methodological template for stress-conditioned genome-to-phenome modeling. [6] takes a different adaptation tack: rather than fine-tuning the foundation model itself, it uses protein-language-model-derived interaction information as an external prior within ICARus, a positive-unlabelled refinement framework that denoises a genome-wide vaccinia-host RNAi screen, sharpening antiviral gene prioritization and releasing both raw and refined read-outs as an agentic-AI-ready community resource. Where [5] emphasizes internal model conditioning, [6] emphasizes read-out post-processing, and the two together demonstrate complementary intervention strategies. Limitations across [3]-[6] include dependence on the coverage and bias of pretraining corpora (particularly salient in [4] for rare antibody classes), the interpretability gap of attention-based motif attribution in [5], and the positive-unlabelled assumption inherent to RNAi screen refinement in [6].

## Databases/Simulation

This category contains a single paper, [7], which exemplifies the database/platform layer that underpins many of the agentic and foundation-model systems described above. TeroSeek is an AI-powered platform that converts terpenoid literature into a structured knowledge base of more than 3 million entries and pairs it with an expert Q&A agent that couples knowledge-graph retrieval with LLM-based answer generation to deliver evidence-based, fully cited responses. The design directly addresses two well-known LLM failure modes in scientific domains—hallucination and knowledge-cutoff lag—by forcing the generation to be grounded in curated literature-derived entries with explicit citations, a strategy that parallels the KG-grounding approaches in [2] and [3]. From a translational perspective, terpenoids constitute a major natural-product class with rich bioactivity and a deep medicinal-chemistry literature, so a domain-specialized platform has clear relevance to drug discovery pipelines. The authors explicitly position the framework as transferable to other scientific domains, suggesting that the knowledge-extraction-and-QA architecture, rather than the terpenoid-specific knowledge base itself, is the primary deliverable—an observation worth emphasizing because it positions [7] as infrastructural rather than purely domain-bound. Limitations include dependence on the completeness and currency of the source literature (an evergreen challenge for any literature-derived KB), potential brittleness to entities or relations not well represented in the source corpus, and the standard caveat that retrieval-grounded answers still require expert verification for high-stakes translational claims. Compared to [1] and [2], which use agents to drive model development and clinical reasoning respectively, [7] uses an agent to deliver expert-style Q&A, demonstrating the breadth of the "agent + KG" template across model-building, clinical evidence, and literature Q&A workflows.
