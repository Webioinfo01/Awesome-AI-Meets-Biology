# Research Paper Report for 2026-09-01:2026-09-15

## Overall Summary

The 2026-09-01 to 2026-09-15 window produced five papers spanning three thematic clusters: AI agents for scientific discovery, benchmarks for knowledge-graph-driven interaction prediction, and foundation model scaling laws for single-cell transcriptomics. The most prominent trend is the convergence of large language models (LLMs) with structured biological knowledge and autonomous experimental workflows. Paper [1] introduces TeroSeek, an AI-powered platform that distills terpenoid literature into a structured knowledge base of over three million entries and couples knowledge-graph retrieval with LLM-based Q&A. This design explicitly targets hallucination reduction and knowledge-cutoff limitations, offering a transferable template for evidence-grounded scientific assistants. Paper [2] shifts attention to agent-driven model development: under a fixed compute budget and human supervision, a general-purpose LLM agent iteratively proposed, implemented, trained, and evaluated changes over 297 iterations, ultimately producing QuickFold, an 8.9M-parameter RNA 3D structure folding trunk that matches open-source baselines such as RhoFold+ and NuFold on lDDT and TM-score within noise while lowering inference cost. Paper [3] presents MErlin, a multi-omics toolkit for bacterial epigenomics delivered both as a Python package and as a Claude agent skill. Its architecture treats natural language as an interface to fixed analytical operations, encoding module-selection rules, preflight checks, and interpretation guidance to avoid ad hoc reimplementation. In the benchmarks cluster, paper [4] describes LLM-HTI, an LLM-driven graph learning framework for herb-macromolecule interaction prediction that fuses latent semantic representations with a Traditional Chinese and Western medicine knowledge graph and employs gradient-guided adaptive parameter updating. Finally, paper [5] examines scaling laws for single-cell RNA sequencing foundation models, showing power-law relationships between pre-training loss, model capacity, and compute, and deriving empirical relationships for optimal learning rate and depth-to-width ratio. Together, these papers signal a maturing ecosystem in which LLMs are no longer merely predictors but also orchestrators, knowledge integrators, and resource-allocation guides. The reliance on preprints for [2], [3], and [5] should be noted, while [1] and [4] appear in established journals. Overall, the field is advancing toward more reproducible, evidence-aware, and compute-efficient AI for scientific discovery.

## AI Agents

This category contains three papers that illustrate complementary uses of LLM agents in biology. Paper [1] focuses on retrieval-augmented knowledge access: TeroSeek converts terpenoid literature into a structured knowledge base and uses knowledge-graph retrieval with LLMs to provide cited, evidence-based answers. This approach reduces hallucination by grounding generation in curated relationships rather than relying solely on parametric memory. Paper [2] explores an agent-led development loop for RNA 3D structure prediction. Instead of using an LLM as a static predictor, the agent proposed, implemented, trained, and evaluated model changes under a budget with human supervision. The resulting QuickFold model matches strong baselines on held-out lDDT and TM-score while using fewer inference resources, though the authors frame the contribution as a case study in feedback-driven development rather than a new state-of-the-art predictor. Paper [3] addresses the data-integration bottleneck in bacterial epigenomics through MErlin, a 17-module toolkit delivered as an agent skill for Claude. The skill encodes procedural rules, mandatory preflight checks, and design-to-inference constraints so that the LLM selects and operates audited software rather than generating new analysis code. Validation on synthetic planted ground truth and a real Pseudoalteromonas haloplanktis methylome-transcriptome comparison supports its reliability. While [1] prioritizes answer quality through knowledge-graph grounding, [3] prioritizes procedural correctness through a fixed module interface, and [2] demonstrates how LLM autonomy can drive model evolution. All three highlight the shift from single-shot inference to iterative, tool-mediated scientific workflows.

## Benchmarks

Paper [4] presents LLM-HTI, a framework for predicting herb-macromolecule target interactions in traditional Chinese medicine. The methodological contribution combines latent semantic representations extracted by LLMs with topological information from a Traditional Chinese and Western medicine knowledge graph (TWKG). This joint characterization enables the model to capture both textual associations and structured biomedical relationships among herbs and macromolecular targets. A notable innovation is the gradient-guided adaptive parameter updating mechanism, which dynamically selects important parameter subsets to improve training stability and optimization efficiency. Evaluation goes beyond standard predictive metrics: gastrointestinal surgeons independently assessed top-ranked candidate targets for disease relevance, biological plausibility, clinical actionability, and translational priority. This clinician-guided evaluation strengthens the translational credibility of the framework and provides a model for integrating domain-expert feedback into computational screening pipelines. Because the paper is classified in the Benchmarks category, it also serves as an evaluation benchmark for LLM-graph hybrid models in herb-target interaction prediction. The authors make data and code publicly available, which facilitates reproducibility and comparative benchmarking. While the paper focuses on traditional Chinese medicine, the underlying graph learning and adaptive parameter methods are potentially generalizable to other multi-component, multi-target therapeutic systems.

## Foundation models

Paper [5] investigates whether empirical scaling laws observed in language and image modeling extend to single-cell transcriptomics. The authors trained foundation models on large collections of single-cell RNA sequencing data and found that pre-training loss decreases systematically with model capacity and training compute, following a power-law dependence on model size. The strength and regularity of these trends vary across model formulations, suggesting that architectural choices influence scaling behavior. Beyond confirming the existence of scaling laws, the study identifies and quantifies empirical relationships linking optimal learning rate and depth-to-width ratio to model size and depth or compute. These relationships provide practical guidance for selecting hyperparameters and architectures when scaling omics foundation models. The work contributes a quantitative framework for estimating expected returns from additional computational resources, which is critical for resource-constrained biological modeling. The preprint’s focus on scaling behavior rather than a single downstream task positions it as a foundational contribution for the broader single-cell and omics AI community. Because the paper does not provide code or public weights, reproducibility will depend on future releases; however, the described methodology offers a template for similar scaling analyses in other biological data modalities.
