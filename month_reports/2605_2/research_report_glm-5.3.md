# Research Paper Report for 2026-05-01:2026-05-31

## Overall Summary

This month's collection comprises ten papers spanning four categories—AI Agents, Benchmarks, Foundation Models, and Reviews—that collectively chart the rapid migration of large language models (LLMs) from general-purpose assistants into core scientific workflows. The single most striking trend is the rise of **agentic and reasoning-based AI systems that produce inspectable, evidence-backed decisions rather than opaque predictions**. SWARM [1] embeds LLM-based behavioral agents inside a biophysical marine model, letting Red Snapper larvae in the Gulf of Mexico "decide" adaptively during the pelagic stage—an approach that overcomes decades-old limits of static trait parameterization. ChatDIA [2] pushes zero-shot LLM reasoning directly onto extracted ion chromatograms in DIA mass spectrometry, matching the domain-specific state-of-the-art DIA-NN (96.9% vs. 95.5% accuracy) while generating human-readable rationales. MyeGPT [3] converts natural-language questions into de novo survival and relapse analyses over the 1,143-patient CoMMpass multiple myeloma cohort, packaged as a smartphone-accessible browser app.

A second theme is the **maturation of biological foundation models**. AMix-2 [5] establishes protein as a native LLM modality via a block-wise diffusion backbone, while OPUS-PLLM [8] closes the gap between generative LLMs and discriminative protein models (ESM2, ProtT5) across 18 benchmarks. structRFM [9] brings structural priors—base-pair interactions via a novel pair-matching operation—to RNA pre-training, achieving ~20% tertiary-structure gains and a 48% F1 improvement on IRES identification. On the applied drug-discovery side, DeepAVC [6] unifies phenotype- and target-based screening and experimentally validates MNS as a broad-spectrum antiviral, whereas the provenance-aware Text-to-Target framework [7] fuses LLM retrieval with omics analytics for Alzheimer's and pancreatic cancer target prioritization with full auditability.

Rigor and realism in evaluation also advance: the ADMET benchmark [4] exposes persistent weaknesses of molecular foundation models (KPGT, Uni-Mol, TabPFNv2) on activity cliffs and out-of-distribution generalization, and Vivien Marx's Nature Methods commentary [10] frames the entire agent wave for the methods community. Methodologically, diffusion-based generation [5], structure-guided masking [9], and modality-aware fusion [7] stand out as innovations with practical, reproducible implications—several releases are fully open-source [5], [9].

## AI Agents

The AI Agents category captures three systems in which LLM-driven agents act as active scientific collaborators rather than passive predictors, each targeting a distinct rung of the biomedical and ecological data stack. SWARM [1] (Simulating Waterborne Agent Routes for Marine connectivity) is the most conceptually novel: it replaces static larval trait parameterizations with LLM-based behavioral agents embedded in a standard biophysical dispersal model, enabling simulated active decision-making during the pelagic larval stage. In both idealized and realistic Gulf of Mexico scenarios for Red Snapper, agents develop adaptive behaviors that improve settlement success and produce explainable vertical distribution patterns—directly informing restoration and connectivity design. ChatDIA [2] operates at the level of raw analytical signal, performing zero-shot, reasoning-based peptide identification over data-independent acquisition MS data. It matched DIA-NN's accuracy on an expert-annotated *Streptococcus pyogenes* benchmark (96.9% vs. 95.5%) and beat it on a challenging single-cell HEK-293T dataset (risk-coverage AUC 0.06 vs. 0.12; 38.75% vs. 16.25% of library peptides at 1% FDR), with conversational validation as a bonus. MyeGPT [3] addresses cohort-scale bioinformatics accessibility, translating natural-language queries into de novo analyses grounded in the 1,143-patient CoMMpass dataset, pro-actively generating visualizations, and shipping as a ready-to-use browser application. While [1] focuses on ecological simulation and [2] on spectrum-level decision-making, [3] completes the spectrum by democratizing population-level omics analytics—united by a shared emphasis on explainability, interactivity, and lowering technical barriers.

## Benchmarks

This month's benchmarking contribution [4] delivers a sorely needed empirical reality check for the foundation-model era in drug discovery. Published in the Journal of Cheminformatics, the study constructs a systematic evaluation framework spanning four real-world ADMET challenges: data scarcity and out-of-distribution (OOD) generalization, class imbalance, "beyond Rule of 5" (bRo5) chemical-space generalization, and activity cliffs. The authors benchmark cutting-edge architectures including molecular foundation models (KPGT, Uni-Mol), the tabular foundation model TabPFNv2, GNNs, AutoML frameworks, and classical machine learning through large-scale train–evaluation cycles. The findings are nuanced and actionable. In few-shot and OOD scenarios, the modality-agnostic TabPFNv2 frequently outperforms purpose-built molecular foundation models—a surprising and humbling result. For extreme class imbalance, undersampling-based ensemble strategies offer a robust recall–precision trade-off. Within complex bRo5 spaces, the pre-trained GNN KPGT achieves the highest accuracy for cyclic peptide permeability given sufficient samples. Critically, all model families consistently fail to capture activity cliffs, where minute structural variations cause dramatic property shifts, exposing a persistent architectural blind spot. Beyond diagnosis, the paper contributes two practical strategies: ensembling high-performance models of different modalities improves predictive robustness, and the molecular property landscape roughness index serves as a lightweight, low-cost diagnostic for pre-screening candidate models for ensemble construction. This complements the agent papers [1]–[3] by clarifying where the underlying predictive models those systems may rely upon remain unreliable.

## Foundation models

The foundation-models category is the largest this month, with five papers spanning proteins, RNA, and drug discovery, and it illustrates two converging strategies: native-modality integration and application-driven LLM frameworks. AMix-2 [5] takes the boldest architectural stance, embedding natural language and protein sequences in a shared token space and employing a block-wise diffusion language modeling backbone that combines causal generation across blocks with bidirectional, iterative refinement within them—better matched to protein structure than strict left-to-right factorization. Its companion benchmark, ProteinArena, introduces time-aware and homology-aware evaluation protocols, and controlled experiments show the diffusion paradigm generally surpasses autoregression. OPUS-PLLM [8] tackles the same protein domain from a different angle, using modality encoding, refinement, and instruction tuning over two new instruction corpora to push generative LLMs past specialized discriminative models on five function-prediction tasks across 18 benchmarks—moving beyond comparisons against general-purpose models like ChatGPT-4o. structRFM [9] extends structural pre-training to RNA, integrating base-pairing into masked language modeling via a novel pair matching operation, mitigating annotation bias with MUSES ensembling, and supporting secondary and tertiary prediction (Zfold) with ~20% gains and AlphaFold3-comparable CASP15/16 and RNA-Puzzles performance, plus a 48% F1 gain on IRES identification; the 21-million sequence-structure dataset is fully open. On the applied side, DeepAVC [6] unifies phenotype-based (DeepPAVC) and target-based (DeepTAVC) antiviral discovery, validated in vitro and in vivo with the identification of MNS as a novel broad-spectrum antiviral outperforming Sisunatovir, while [7] contributes a provenance-aware Text-to-Target framework whose modality-aware fusion of LLM retrieval with omics analytics achieved DepMap-supported target and strategy enrichment in PDAC and Alzheimer's disease with end-to-end auditability.

## Reviews

The Reviews category contains a single but strategically significant entry: Vivien Marx's commentary "Call your AI agent," published in Nature Methods [10]. Appearing in one of the premier methodology venues, this piece (already accruing citations) addresses the scientific community's growing use of AI agents for research—an editorial framing that arrives at precisely the moment the empirical landscape demonstrates concrete, working systems. The commentary's topical relevance is underscored by this month's agent contributions: SWARM's LLM-driven larval behavioral agents [1], ChatDIA's zero-shot reasoning over DIA mass spectrometry chromatograms [2], and MyeGPT's natural-language bioinformatics over the CoMMpass myeloma cohort [3]. Where those papers showcase capability, [10] provides the methodological-community perspective on adoption, positioned within the same collection as rigorous reliability benchmarking [4] and foundation-model advances across proteins [5], [8], RNA [9], and drug discovery [6], [7]. As a commentary rather than a primary research article, no abstract was provided, but its inclusion reflects the recognition that agentic AI has crossed from proof-of-concept into the mainstream methods discourse. For readers tracking the trajectory of AI-for-science, this piece offers high-level context from Nature Methods that complements the technical depth of the surrounding primary literature, and it signals that venue-level conversations about evaluation standards, reproducibility, and responsible deployment of scientific agents are now underway alongside the systems themselves.
