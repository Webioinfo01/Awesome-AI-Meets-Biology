# Research Paper Report for 2026-09-16:2026-09-30

## Overall Summary

This bi-weekly window (September 16–30, 2026) captures a fascinating inflection point in the deployment of large language model (LLM)-driven systems for scientific discovery, with five papers spanning AI agents for bioinformatics, synthetic biology, genomics, scientific publishing, and de novo drug design. A unifying theme is the move from passive prediction to **active, workflow-controlling AI agents** that not only generate outputs but also orchestrate tools, decide what to analyze next, and produce actionable artifacts for human researchers.

The dominant methodological thread is **agentic decoupling of orchestration from adjudication**. The most striking finding comes from [3], which demonstrates that the same GPT-5.6 Sol model that excels as a workflow controller (matching exhaustive fixed strategies at 18/20 accuracy while using 32–47% fewer follow-up analyses) collapses in accuracy to 11/20 when granted final biological decision authority. This empirical dissociation suggests that current LLMs are reliable routers but unreliable interpreters of biological evidence—a critical distinction for clinical and functional genomics pipelines. Similarly, [1] evaluates three coding agents (Biomni, Claude Code, Codex) on a CUT&Tag Nextflow pipeline and finds that all three produce superficially plausible but functionally incomplete workflows; notably, none generated the requested merged-group reports, and sample-level MultiQC outputs diverged from a hand-coded reference. [1] and [3] together argue that current agents are best understood as **accelerators for expert users**, not autonomous replacements, and that prompts must encode stage semantics, acceptance tests, and failure criteria.

A second theme is **infrastructure for agent-mediated science**. [4] introduces Paper2Agent (published in *Nature*), a framework that converts research papers into Model Context Protocol (MCP) servers, effectively turning manuscripts into "virtual corresponding authors" that expose code, data, and methods as agent-native tools. Case studies span AlphaGenome for variant interpretation and Scanpy/TISSUE for spatial transcriptomics, with multi-agent collaboration demonstrated on psoriasis gene prioritization. [2] presents LabscriptAI, an execution-aware harness that translates natural language into platform-specific robot scripts while enforcing biosecurity, community standards, and human oversight. On a 90-task liquid-handling benchmark, LabscriptAI achieved a 96.7% simulation-pass rate and supported real-world deployments including 854 GFP designs, a 6.7-fold-improved formaldehyde-converting enzyme cascade, and 531 quality-assured parts for the 2025 iGEM Distribution Kit.

Finally, [5] extends the LLM-for-science paradigm to **generative chemistry**, integrating a 70B-parameter Llama-family model with the Boltz-2 co-folding diffusion model in a feedback loop that optimizes predicted binding affinity and synthetic accessibility. Validated on ACVR1 (fibrodysplasia ossificans progressiva) and CD19 (B-cell malignancies), LLMsFold illustrates how foundation models and biophysical simulators can be composed into closed-loop design systems.

Taken together, the papers highlight rapid progress in **agent scaffolding** and **tool-use protocols**, but also underscore persistent gaps in **biological judgment**, **numerical correctness**, and **end-to-end reliability**—suggesting that near-term impact will come from tightly scoped, human-supervised agent deployments rather than fully autonomous scientific reasoning.

## AI Agents

This category contains four papers that interrogate the deployment of LLM-based agents across distinct scientific workflows: bioinformatics pipeline construction [1], laboratory robotic automation in synthetic biology [2], bacterial gene annotation [3], and the conversion of research papers into interactive agents [4]. Together, they delineate both the structural maturity and the current limitations of agentic systems for scientific discovery.

[1] provides a rigorous comparative evaluation of Biomni, Claude Code, and Codex on a single, well-defined task: constructing a Nextflow DSL2 pipeline for paired-end CUT&Tag data covering QC, trimming, alignment, filtering, peak calling, annotation, FRiP, deepTools visualization, and MultiQC reporting. All three agents produced plausible-looking file structures and documentation, but none satisfied the requested analysis: group-level merging outputs were universally missing, and sample-level MultiQC reports diverged from a hand-coded reference. Codex showed the closest alignment on primary mapped-read counts; Claude Code produced the broadest but numerically inconsistent report; Biomni offered the strongest subjective documentation but the weakest quantitative agreement. The authors conclude that agents are powerful scaffolding tools but require expert review to distinguish correct from merely plausible pipelines, and that prompts must encode stage semantics, metadata contracts, and acceptance tests.

[2] advances the state of the art in **execution-aware** agent systems through LabscriptAI, which couples authoring and runtime control loops to translate natural language into platform-specific robot scripts under deterministic authorization and human oversight. LabscriptAI achieved a 96.7% simulation-pass rate on a 90-task liquid-handling benchmark—the highest among LLM-based and commercial baselines—and was deployed in real-world scenarios including sequence-level biosecurity screening, standardized characterization of 854 GFP designs from 171 student teams, containment-aware engineering of a formaldehyde-converting enzyme (yielding 6.7-fold higher ethylene glycol titers), and quality-assured preparation of 531 parts for the 2025 iGEM Distribution Kit. The framework explicitly addresses four requirements unique to synthetic biology: sequence-level biosecurity, community standards, context-dependent biological behavior, and distributed multi-laboratory collaboration.

[3] isolates the **orchestration versus adjudication** question using Genome Skeptic, a deterministic bacterial gene-annotation framework in which all measurements are produced by fixed bioinformatics tools and an AI controller can only request follow-ups from a predefined registry. In a prospectively locked, blinded cohort of 20 genomes balanced for tet(A)/tet(B) presence and absence, GPT-5.6 Sol as workflow controller matched exhaustive strategies at 18/20 accuracy while consuming 32–47% fewer follow-up analyses. A 60-case prospective benchmark confirmed that divergent evidence-acquisition policies converged on identical endpoint calls. However, when the same Sol model was granted final decision authority, accuracy dropped to 11/20 (McNemar P = 0.016), specificity fell from 1.00 to 0.30, and all nine disagreements shifted negative calls to "unresolved" rather than recovering missed positives. This provides direct empirical evidence that orchestration competence does not entail biological adjudication competence.

[4], published in *Nature*, introduces **Paper2Agent**, which converts research papers into MCP servers that expose manuscript, supplementary materials, datasets, code, and workflows as agent-native tools. The framework analyzes papers and codebases using multiple agents to construct the MCP, then generates and runs tests for robustness refinement. Connected to chat agents such as Claude Code, these paper MCPs enable natural-language scientific queries that invoke paper-specific tools. Demonstrations include an AlphaGenome-based variant interpretation agent, Scanpy- and TISSUE-based single-cell and spatial transcriptomics agents, and a multi-agent collaboration that prioritized a causal psoriasis gene. While [1], [2], and [3] focus on whether agents can correctly perform bounded scientific tasks, [4] addresses the **dissemination layer**, turning static publications into reusable, queryable scientific infrastructure.

Across these four works, a coherent picture emerges: agents are reliable when given **bounded tool registries** and **deterministic evaluators** ([3]), can scale to real laboratory deployments when wrapped in **execution-aware harnesses** ([2]), can scaffold complex code when supervised by experts ([1]), and can transform the scientific literature itself when given structured protocols ([4]). The principal unresolved challenge is reliable biological judgment in open-ended decision contexts.

## Other Models

This category contains a single paper [5] that demonstrates how large language models can be integrated with biophysical simulation foundations for generative drug design. Whereas the AI Agents category emphasizes orchestration and tool-use protocols around existing scientific software, this work illustrates a complementary paradigm in which an LLM directly proposes candidate molecules and a biophysical foundation model (Boltz-2) validates them.

[5] introduces **LLMsFold**, a computational framework that combines a 70-billion-parameter Llama-family transformer with geometry-based pocket detection and the Boltz-2 diffusion model for protein–ligand co-folding and binding-affinity prediction. The pipeline first identifies viable binding pockets on the target, then uses prompt-constrained SMILES generation to enforce drug-likeness, and finally evaluates candidates through Boltz-2 in an iterative feedback loop that prioritizes predicted affinity and synthetic accessibility. Two challenging therapeutic targets are used as validation: ACVR1 (implicated in fibrodysplasia ossificans progressiva) and CD19 (a surface antigen on most B-cell lymphomas and leukemias). Top candidates exhibit strong in silico binding predictions and favorable drug-like profiles, and all code and models are released to support reproducibility.

The methodological contrast with the AI Agents category is instructive: [1], [2], [3], and [4] deploy agents as **controllers** over existing deterministic tools, while [5] deploys an LLM as a **generator** whose outputs are filtered by a downstream biophysical model. Both paradigms share the principle of bounding LLM outputs through structured evaluators—whether Nextflow execution semantics ([1]), robot-script runtime ([2]), fixed evidence registries ([3]), MCP tool interfaces ([4]), or co-folding binding predictions ([5]). This compositional pattern—LLM proposal plus deterministic validation—appears to be the dominant near-term architecture for responsibly deploying foundation models in science.
