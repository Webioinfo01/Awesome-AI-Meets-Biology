# Research Paper Report for 2026-05-01:2026-05-31

## Overall Summary

This reporting period (May 2026) showcases a research landscape increasingly defined by the migration of large language models (LLMs) and AI agents from general-purpose dialogue into the core infrastructure of scientific discovery, clinical practice, and molecular biology. A dominant theme across the fourteen papers collected here is the use of LLMs not merely as predictors, but as **reasoning engines and orchestrators** embedded within domain-specific workflows. In the AI agents category, ChatDIA [5] demonstrates that zero-shot LLM reasoning can competitively automate targeted DIA proteomics analysis (96.9% accuracy on an expert-annotated *Streptococcus pyogenes* benchmark, matching DIA-NN's 95.5%), while AERO [4] applies agentic LLM classification of clinical trial eligibility criteria to real-world trial emulation of the WARCEF trial. LEHCA [2] elevates LLMs to a coarse-timescale "Commander" role in hierarchical multi-agent reinforcement learning, grounding semantic sub-goals into QMIX-based low-level agents via semantic reward shaping and dynamic action masking. Complementing these, TRUST [1] targets trauma mental health dialogue assessment, and an AI-agent-orchestrated grey-box Transformer [3] addresses sparse pharmacokinetic curve reconstruction.

The foundation models category reveals a parallel push toward *biologically native* pretraining architectures. AMix-2 [8] embeds protein sequences in a shared token space with natural language using block-wise diffusion modeling, while OPUS-PLLM [11] closes the gap between generative LLMs and discriminative specialists (ESM2, ProtT5) across 18 protein function benchmarks, and structRFM [12] integrates RNA base-pairing structure into masked language modeling with a fully open 21-million sequence-structure dataset. DeepAVC [9] unifies phenotype- and target-based antiviral discovery, validated in vitro and in vivo, identifying MNS as a broad-spectrum antiviral. Beyond biology, LLM-UTP [10] scales city-wide traffic prediction across 29 cities.

Benchmarking work provides critical reality checks: [6] exposes persistent failures of molecular and tabular foundation models on activity cliffs and OOD ADMET scenarios, while TCM-3CEval [7] reveals culturally grounded deficits in clinical TCM reasoning. Policy reviews [13], [14] round out the month with FDA regulatory perspectives and a Nature Methods primer on scientific AI agents.

## AI Agents

The five papers in this category collectively illustrate the breadth of agentic LLM architectures, spanning mental healthcare, cooperative control, pharmacometrics, clinical trial methodology, and mass spectrometry. Methodologically, the common thread is the decomposition of expert judgment into explicit, LLM-executable reasoning steps. TRUST [1] develops an LLM-based dialogue system for trauma understanding and structured clinical assessment, published in JAMIA, a leading Q1 medical informatics venue—emphasizing the clinical validation rigor required for mental health deployments. LEHCA [2] takes a contrasting approach: rather than dialogue, it positions an LLM as a hierarchical "Commander" that converts structured textual environment summaries into strategic sub-goals, semantic reward-shaping rules, and action-level constraints for QMIX-based agents. Across eight StarCraft multi-agent challenge scenarios it outperforms QMIX, QPLEX, MAVEN, and MAPPO in early-stage learning efficiency, with ablations confirming both hierarchical guidance and LLM semantic reasoning contribute. While [2] focuses on simulated cooperative control, AERO [4] and [3] address biomedical data problems: AERO integrates external clinical knowledge with LLM reasoning to classify trial eligibility criteria (strict inclusion, safety exclusion, confounder, operational artifact), emulating WARCEF with an HR of 1.561 (p = 0.0605) consistent with the original neutral trial; ablations showed eligibility handling materially shifts treatment effects. Paper [3] orchestrates a grey-box Transformer for sparse pharmacokinetic curve reconstruction and pharmacometric initialization. ChatDIA [5] arguably pushes furthest methodologically, achieving zero-shot peptide identification directly over extracted ion chromatograms with human-interpretable rationales, beating DIA-NN on single-cell HEK-293T data (risk-coverage AUC 0.06 vs. 0.12). Practical implications span conversational proteomics exploration, trial generalizability, and interpretable clinical assessment.

## Benchmarks

This month's benchmarking contributions provide sobering, empirically grounded assessments of where foundation models genuinely excel versus where marketing claims outpace capability. The ADMET reliability study [6] is the more methodologically expansive of the two, constructing a testing framework around four real-world drug discovery challenges: data scarcity and out-of-distribution (OOD) generalization, class imbalance, "beyond Rule of 5" (bRo5) chemical space generalization, and activity cliffs. Its large-scale train–evaluation cycles compare molecular foundation models (KPGT, Uni-Mol), the tabular foundation model TabPFNv2, GNNs, AutoML, and classical ML. Notable findings include TabPFNv2's surprising superiority in few-shot/OOD regimes, the effectiveness of undersampling-based ensembles under extreme class imbalance, and KPGT's strength on cyclic peptide permeability within bRo5 space—yet all architectures consistently fail on activity cliffs, where minute structural variations cause dramatic property shifts. The study also validates the molecular property landscape roughness index as a lightweight, low-cost diagnostic for model selection. While [6] probes quantitative molecular modeling limits, TCM-3CEval [7] addresses culturally grounded medical reasoning, evaluating 450 expert-validated single-choice questions across three dimensions—fundamental knowledge, classical text comprehension, and clinical decision-making—using permutation-based consistency testing to control positional bias. Models trained with Chinese linguistic and cultural priors outperform international counterparts on classical texts, but pronounced deficits persist in TCM Diagnostics and Syndrome Differentiation, distinguishing rote memorization from holistic clinical inference. Together, these benchmarks establish that evaluation rigor—whether chemical [6] or cultural [7]—remains essential for responsible deployment.

## Foundation models

The five foundation model papers this month span protein, RNA, antiviral small molecules, and urban traffic systems, unified by the goal of making pretrained models both more biologically/domain-native and more generalizable. AMix-2 [8] makes the most architecturally ambitious claim: establishing protein as a *native modality* within LLMs via a shared token space unifying understanding and conditional design, powered by a block-wise diffusion backbone combining causal inter-block generation with bidirectional intra-block refinement—better matching protein structure than strict left-to-right factorization. Its companion benchmark, ProteinArena, introduces time-aware and homology-aware protocols, showing AMix-2 outperforming frontier LLMs and competing with task-specific protein models. OPUS-PLLM [11] complements [8] by targeting the sequence-to-function paradigm through modality encoding, refinement, and instruction tuning, trained on OPUS-InstructionCorpus and OPUS-InstructionCorpus-Evol across six functional annotations; it surpasses both biological-knowledge-integrated LLMs and specialized discriminative models (ESM2, ProtT5) on most of 18 benchmarks. While [8] and [11] focus on proteins, structRFM [12] extends structural pretraining to RNA, integrating base pairing into masked language modeling via a novel pair matching operation, with MUSES ensemble secondary structures and dynamic masking; it achieves ~20% gains in tertiary structure prediction and 48% F1 gains on internal ribosome entry site identification. DeepAVC [9] couples phenotype-based (DeepPAVC) and target-based (DeepTAVC) LLM screening, validated in vitro/in vivo and identifying MNS as a broad-spectrum antiviral surpassing Sisunatovir. LLM-UTP [10] demonstrates domain generality beyond biology, validated on 11 datasets across 29 cities. Practical limitations remain, notably dataset scale constraints and benchmark coverage gaps.

## Reviews

The two review contributions this month address complementary governance and adoption questions raised by the technical advances documented in the preceding categories. The npj Digital Medicine piece [13], the second in a three-article interview series with Dr. Shantanu Nundy—an advisor in the office of the Commissioner of the US FDA on AI governance and digital health policy—examines the challenge of regulating LLM-enabled medical AI that extends beyond the usual boundaries of medical devices and therefore strains traditional regulatory frameworks. Its significance lies in grounding abstract policy debates in the practical realities faced by regulators confronting systems like the clinical dialogue agents exemplified by TRUST [1] or the trial emulation workflows of AERO [4], where LLM behavior is probabilistic, context-dependent, and difficult to frame as a fixed medical device. This regulatory lens pairs naturally with the empirical findings of TCM-3CEval [7], which demonstrated clinically unreliable subdomain performance—precisely the failure modes regulators must anticipate. Meanwhile, the Nature Methods commentary "Call your AI agent" [14] offers a methods-focused perspective on AI agents in scientific research, directly relevant to the agentic workflows of ChatDIA [5] and the grey-box pharmacokinetic orchestrator [3], which move beyond prediction toward autonomous decision-making. Together, these reviews frame the month's technical progress: [13] asks whether existing US regulatory scaffolding can accommodate LLM healthcare deployments, while [14] surveys how laboratory scientists should engage with increasingly autonomous computational collaborators. Both implicitly acknowledge a shared limitation of the field—lagging governance and methodological norms relative to capability growth.
