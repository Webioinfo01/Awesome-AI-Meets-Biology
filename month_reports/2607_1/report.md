# Research Paper Report for 2026-07-01:2026-07-15

## Overall Summary

This report synthesizes four research papers published between July 1 and July 15, 2026, spanning two categories: **AI Agents** (3 papers) and **Foundation models** (1 paper). Despite the modest volume, the collection captures a defining trend in computational biology and drug discovery: the migration from implicit human expertise toward explicitly encoded, machine-readable knowledge that large language models (LLMs) can operationalize within scientific workflows.

In the AI Agents category, [1] presents a rigorous single-target case study in which a Claude Code agent, guided by a Markdown "skill file," orchestrates physics-based (Uni-Dock) and diffusion-based (DiffDock v1) docking for formyl peptide receptor 2 (FPR2) virtual screening. The skill-guided protocol improved global ranking (ROC AUC 0.701 → 0.733, DeLong P = 3.4 × 10⁻³) while degrading early enrichment (EF@0.5% 3.33 → 0.00), with DiffDock v1 remaining near-random (AUC 0.54–0.56) on this out-of-distribution GPCR—a candid assessment of bundled interventions and their limitations. Complementing this, [2] introduces OCellus, a language-model framework applying natural-language reasoning to single-cell, spatial, and perturbation biology, while [3] describes cGSA, an LLM-augmented gene set analysis framework that incorporates clinical context into pathway prioritization, outperforming baseline methods by over 30% on 102 curated gene sets across 19 diseases with expert validation.

In the Foundation models category, [4] unveils NEVA, a multimodal vision-language foundation model for neuroblastoma precision oncology. Trained and evaluated on a 1,238-patient multi-institutional cohort, NEVA employs a pathologist-inspired hierarchical workflow with end-to-end optimization, outperforming ten representative foundation models (including TITAN, UNI, and Virchow) across 11 clinical tasks—reaching AUROCs of 0.916 for subtype classification and 0.924 for NMYC amplification prediction.

Collectively, these papers demonstrate escalating technical sophistication in evaluation methodology (paired bias-corrected and accelerated bootstrap, DeLong tests, permutation testing, expert validation) and a shared emphasis on interpretability, reproducibility, and open data release, signaling maturation in AI-driven biomedical research.

## AI Agents

The AI Agents category this period comprises three papers united by the application of large language models as reasoning and coordination engines for biomedical analysis, rather than as standalone predictors. Paper [1] contributes the most methodologically transparent study: a single-target FPR2 virtual screening case study in which a Claude Code agent curated 1,000 pChEMBL ≥5 actives and 9,829 property-matched decoys, then executed naive versus skill-file-guided ligand preparation and docking protocols across two engines—Uni-Dock (GPU-accelerated AutoDock Vina) and DiffDock v1. The evaluation is statistically rigorous, deploying ROC AUC, Boltzmann-enhanced discrimination of ROC, enrichment factors, and paired bias-corrected and accelerated bootstrap, DeLong, and permutation tests. The authors honestly attribute the +0.020 AUC gain to a bundled preparation-and-docking-parameter intervention rather than the skill file alone, noting single-run, no-seed-control limitations.

While [1] encodes expert protocol knowledge for molecular docking, [3] improves on the context-blindness problem in gene set analysis by integrating gene cluster detection, enrichment analysis, and LLMs for context-aware pathway prioritization—benchmarking on 102 curated gene sets across 19 diseases and ten biological mechanisms, achieving over 30% improvement over baselines with expert validation and melanoma/breast cancer case studies. Paper [2] extends the agent paradigm further into interactive territory with OCellus, a natural-language reasoning framework spanning single-cell, spatial, and perturbation biology—though as a bioRxiv preprint it lacks the peer-reviewed detail of [1] (Computational and Structural Biotechnology Journal) and [3] (Bioinformatics, already citing 1 citation). Together, these works chart a progression from protocol guidance [1], to contextual interpretation [3], to full analytical frameworks [2].

## Foundation Models

The Foundation models category is represented by a single but substantial contribution: NEVA (NEuroblastoma Vision–language AI), paper [4], published in Nature Communications. NEVA addresses the persistent clinical challenge of neuroblastoma—a leading cause of childhood cancer mortality—by targeting biological heterogeneity and the limited accessibility of molecular profiling in routine practice. Its architectural distinction from conventional computational pathology approaches is significant: rather than relying on frozen encoders paired with multiple instance learning, NEVA implements a pathologist-inspired hierarchical workflow with end-to-end optimization, positioning it as a true multimodal foundation model rather than a pipeline of disconnected components.

The empirical evaluation is extensive. Developed and assessed across a large multi-institutional cohort of 1,238 patients, NEVA outperforms ten representative foundation models—including TITAN, UNI, and Virchow—across the majority of 11 clinical tasks. Quantitatively, it achieves AUROCs of 0.916 for subtype classification, 0.823 for Shimada classification, and 0.806 for risk group stratification. Critically, the model predicts key molecular alterations from routinely available pathology data, reaching 0.924 AUROC for NMYC amplification and 0.830 for 1p36 deletion—capabilities that could substitute for expensive molecular assays in resource-limited settings. NEVA further enables prognostic stratification for progression-free and overall survival across multiple test cohorts, and integrates interpretable attention maps that localize histologically relevant regions, addressing the black-box concern that often limits clinical adoption. While the model's scope is necessarily specialized to neuroblastoma, its framework—end-to-end multimodal optimization with clinical interpretability—offers a scalable template for risk stratification and decision support in other pediatric and adult cancers, and its benchmarking against established pathology foundation models (TITAN, UNI, Virchow) situates it firmly at the state of the art.
